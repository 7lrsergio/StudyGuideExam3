01 · Team characteristics

Teams, slides 3–30

A team is two or more people working interdependently over time toward a common, task-oriented goal. A collection of people is not automatically a team: look for shared goals and dependence on each other.

Input → Process → Output
Inputs: team type, development, interdependence, composition.
Processes: conflict, cooperation, coordination, teamwork.
Outputs: performance, satisfaction, viability (ability to keep working together), networking.

Identify the team by its purpose

TypePurpose / distinguishing clueExample
WorkOngoing goods or services; long life, high involvementProduction or sales team
ManagementIntegrates activities across business functions; long life, moderate involvementDepartment heads coordinating the organization
ParallelRecommendations or problem solving alongside regular work; involvement usually lowSafety committee
ProjectA defined, one-time output; ends when the assignment endsSix-month check-in redesign ★
ActionComplex tasks under visible or challenging conditionsSurgical team or sports team
PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Soccer: same sport, different team types

Imagine a club’s players competing in a high-pressure match: an action team. A temporary group redesigning its stadium app is a project team. A safety committee advising the club while keeping regular jobs is a parallel team. The sport does not determine the category; the group’s purpose does.

Remember: Ask what the team was created to do.

Development: linear vs. punctuated equilibrium

Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing → Adjourning

Forming: learn boundaries and expectations. Storming: competing ideas and conflict. Norming: cooperation and shared expectations emerge. Performing: roles work smoothly and the team advances its goal. Adjourning: separation and possible anxiety. This sequence is useful but does not fit every team.

Punctuated equilibrium: initial patterns → inertia → midpoint process revision → another period of inertia. The key clue is a major change around the halfway point.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Naruto: the group-project version

Imagine Naruto, Sasuke and Sakura assigned a class presentation. First they ask who does what (forming); then argue over the plan (storming); agree on rules (norming); work smoothly (performing); and split up after presenting (adjourning). If they coast until halfway through the deadline and suddenly overhaul the plan, that is punctuated equilibrium.

Remember: Stages describe the journey; a midpoint reset signals punctuated equilibrium.

Three kinds of interdependence

Goal: individual goals align with a shared vision. Outcome: members share rewards. Task: work itself requires interaction.

Task patternWhat happensRecognition cue
Pooled ★Independent work is combinedSeparate pastry batches added to a total; least coordination
SequentialWork passes in a fixed orderAssembly line: A → B → C
ReciprocalMembers exchange work back and forthA ↔ B, then a handoff to another part of the team
ComprehensiveEveryone interacts flexibly and extensivelyWhole team continually iterates together; most coordination

Do not confuse a shared bonus (outcome interdependence) with the pattern of work (task interdependence).

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Messi and Ronaldo: follow the ball—or the work

Imagine four drills. Each player independently takes shots and totals are combined: pooled. Ronaldo passes to Messi, who passes to a designated finisher in a fixed order: sequential. Messi and Ronaldo exchange the ball back and forth before passing it onward: reciprocal. The entire squad continually adjusts and exchanges passes with one another: comprehensive. These simplified drills illustrate work patterns; a real match can contain several patterns.

Remember: Separate totals → fixed chain → back-and-forth → everyone adapting.

Composition: who is on the team?

Size: more people can help complex management/project work, but can create coordination problems in routine work teams. Roles: the responsibilities members take on. Diversity: surface differences include age, race, ethnicity and gender; deep differences include values, personality, interests, knowledge and skills. The slides say negative surface-level effects tend to fade over time, while negative deep-level effects can grow as differences emerge.

Ability patternPerformance depends on…Example
AdditiveThe sum of everyone’s contributionsTotal team sales
DisjunctiveThe most capable member / best solutionSolving a problem with one objectively best answer
ConjunctiveThe weakest linkMountain-climbing team

Personality: one low-conscientiousness member can hurt the team. Agreeableness supports cooperation and trust, but can suppress useful task conflict. Extraversion can help, although too many dominant members may create power struggles.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Dragon Ball: three ways to score a training challenge

Imagine Goku’s group earns points by adding everyone’s individual scores: additive. One person solving a puzzle wins for the whole group: disjunctive. Everyone must cross a course together, so the slowest member limits completion: conjunctive.

Remember: Additive = add everyone. Disjunctive = best solution. Conjunctive = weakest link.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Soccer: goal, outcome and task are different

Imagine Messi and Ronaldo agree that every personal training target should support winning the tournament: goal interdependence. The squad shares a tournament bonus: outcome interdependence. Their passing and positioning depend on teammates’ actions: task interdependence. A single team can have all three.

Remember: Goal = shared aim. Outcome = shared reward. Task = connected work.

02 · Team processes

Teams, slides 35–49

Actual performance = potential performance + process gains − process losses.
Gains come from cooperation and knowledge sharing; simply adding people does not guarantee a gain.
LossMeaningClue
Coordination lossCosts of coordinating teammates’ workTime/effort spent aligning activities
Production blocking ★Waiting for others before doing your part“I cannot start until they finish”
Motivational lossMembers do not work as hard as they couldReduced effort
Social loafingLess effort in a team than when doing the same task alone“Others will cover my share”
PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Chainsaw Man: the presentation is due tomorrow

Imagine Denji cannot create the final slides until Power sends her section: production blocking. Now imagine Denji could work but contributes less because “Aki will handle it”: social loafing. The first problem is waiting; the second is reduced effort in the group.

Remember: Cannot proceed vs. will not put in the usual effort.

Taskwork vs. teamwork vs. states

Taskwork directly accomplishes the job: creative behavior, decision making, boundary spanning. Teamwork supports the interpersonal setting for that work: transition, action and interpersonal processes, including communication. States are shared feelings and thoughts, rather than activities.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Soccer: playing, coordinating and believing

In an imagined training session, taking a shot directly performs the task: taskwork. Teammates discussing how to coordinate supports the work: teamwork. Their shared confidence that they can handle many opponents is a team state, specifically potency.

Remember: Doing the task → supporting the team → shared thoughts or feelings.

Nominal group technique

Outline purpose → Write ideas alone → Round-robin sharing → Clarify/build → Rank privately → Tally

Generate many ideas, avoid early criticism, and build on others’ ideas. Private idea generation reduces waiting and gives every member a voice.

Decision quality factorQuestion to ask
Decision informityDo members have adequate task information?
Staff validityAre members making good recommendations?
Hierarchical sensitivityDoes the leader weigh recommendations effectively?
Boundary-spanning activityWhat crosses the boundary?
AmbassadorSupport, protection, and resources for the team
Task coordinatorTask coordination with other functional areas
ScoutInformation on technology, competitors, or markets
Team stateMeaning / key distinction
CohesionEmotional bond; supports motivation and commitment, but may encourage conformity
PotencyShared confidence the team can succeed across situations
Mental modelsShared understanding of the team and its tasks
Transactive memoryIntegrated specialized knowledge: knowing who knows what

Groupthink warning signs: over-optimism/invulnerability, stereotypes of outsiders, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity, pressure on dissenters. Agreement alone is not proof of a good decision.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Jujutsu Kaisen: a quieter idea can still win

Imagine Yuji, Megumi and Nobara planning a campus event. Each writes ideas privately, everyone shares in turns, the group clarifies ideas, and each ranks them privately. A facilitator totals the rankings. That is nominal group technique: the loudest voice does not automatically decide the winner.

Remember: Write → share → clarify → rank → tally.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Naruto: who knows what?

Imagine everyone in Naruto’s group understands the mission plan and roles: mental models. They know Sakura has the medical information and another member has navigation expertise: transactive memory. They feel emotionally close: cohesion. They believe the group can succeed across challenges: potency.

Remember: Plan in common / expertise directory / emotional bond / shared confidence.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Messi: a great idea still needs a good decision process

Imagine players advise a coach about an opponent. Having enough information is decision informity. Giving sound recommendations is staff validity. The coach weighing those recommendations properly is hierarchical sensitivity. Even if Messi gives excellent advice, the final decision can fail if the coach weighs the advice badly.

Remember: Information → recommendations → leader’s weighting.

03 · Power, influence & conflict

Leadership Part 1, slides 4–26

Leadership: using power and influence to direct followers toward goals. Power is the capacity to influence; influence is an actual behavior that changes attitudes or behavior. Influence can be downward, lateral, or upward.

Power baseSourceScenario clue
Legitimate · organizationalFormal authority“I am your supervisor”
Reward · organizationalControl over desired rewardsRaises or desirable assignments
Coercive · organizationalControl over punishmentThreat of firing or demotion
Expert · personalValuable knowledge or skill“They know how to solve this”
Referent · personal ★Admiration, affection, loyalty“I want to be like them”
PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Ronaldo and Messi: why are people listening?

Imagine a youth player follows Ronaldo because they admire him: referent power. They follow Messi’s advice because it contains valuable technical knowledge: expert power. A coach’s official authority is legitimate power; control over a desired starting spot is reward power; control over a disciplinary suspension is coercive power. The same person can have several bases.

Remember: Admiration ≠ expertise. Identify the reason for following.

When power becomes stronger

Low substitutability ★: few alternatives to the person’s resources. Centrality: others depend on the person’s work. Discretion: freedom to make decisions. Visibility: others know about the person’s resources or contribution.

More substitutes means less dependence and less power. Expert power is knowledge; referent power is admiration.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Jujutsu Kaisen: the only person with the key

Imagine Megumi alone can unlock the team’s training room. Few substitutes increase his power through low substitutability. Everyone needs the room, making his role central. He can approve access himself: discretion. Everyone knows he controls access: visibility. If ten other people can unlock it, his substitutability rises and his power falls.

Remember: Hard to replace, important to others, free to decide, known to matter.

Influence tactics

Effectiveness in the slidesTactic → recognition cue
Most effectiveRational persuasion: facts and logic.
Consultation: involve target in planning.
Inspirational appeals: appeal to values/emotions.
Collaboration: supply help/resources.
Moderately effectiveIngratiation: favors/compliments.
Personal appeals: friendship.
Exchange: offer a reward for compliance.
Apprising: explain personal benefits of the request.
Least effectivePressure: threats/demands.
Coalitions: recruit others to influence the target.

Internalization: agrees and commits; attitude + behavior change. Compliance: does it without enthusiasm; behavior change only (most common response in the slides). Resistance: opposes or avoids; neither intended attitude nor behavior change.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Naruto: three reactions to the same request

Imagine Kakashi asks the group to train earlier. Naruto believes the change is useful and commits: internalization. Sasuke attends but privately thinks it is pointless: compliance. Another member refuses and avoids the session: resistance. The request is identical; the response differs.

Remember: Believe and do / just do / oppose or avoid.

Conflict styles: whose outcomes matter?

StyleConcern for own outcomesConcern for othersResult shorthand
CompeteHighLowWin–lose
CollaborateHighHighWin–win
AvoidLowLowLose–lose
AccommodateLowHighLose–win
CompromiseModerateModerateBoth give something up

Task conflict can help if managed effectively; relationship conflict tends to hurt. Collaborating searches for a solution meeting both parties’ concerns; compromising involves concessions.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Chainsaw Man: getting Denji to help

Imagine Aki asks Denji to help set up an event. “Here is evidence that this plan saves time” is rational persuasion. “Help decide how we do it” is consultation. “I will bring tools and help you” is collaboration. “I will buy you lunch if you help” is exchange. “This will teach you useful skills” is apprising.

Remember: Help with the task = collaboration. Offer a reward = exchange. Explain its benefit = apprising.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Dragon Ball: two people, one training room

Imagine Goku and Vegeta want the same room. One insists on getting it: compete. One gives it up: accommodate. Both ignore the dispute: avoid. Each gives up half the desired time: compromise. They find a schedule that fully meets both training needs: collaborate.

Remember: Compromise splits the sacrifice; collaboration looks for a solution satisfying both.

04 · Leadership behaviors & styles

Leadership Part 2, slides 3–32

Emergence: who becomes a leader. Effectiveness: whether actions achieve goals, build employee commitment, and develop quality relationships. Becoming a leader does not guarantee effectiveness.

The slide’s trait table links openness, extraversion, cognitive ability, energy, stress tolerance and self-confidence to both emergence and effectiveness. High conscientiousness and low agreeableness are marked for emergence; low neuroticism for effectiveness.

Leader–member exchange (LMX): quality of each leader–employee relationship. High-quality dyads exchange more information, influence, latitude, support and attention; they are associated with higher performance, satisfaction and leader-effectiveness ratings.

Daily behaviorWhat to recognize
Initiating structure ★Clarifies roles, organizes work, schedules tasks, sets goals and standards
ConsiderationBuilds trust/respect, listens, supports welfare, recognizes people and represents the group
PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Soccer coach: organize the drill or support the player?

Imagine a coach tells Ronaldo exactly which drill to run, when to start and what target to meet: initiating structure. The coach listens to Messi’s concerns, treats him respectfully and supports his well-being: consideration. A leader can do both; they are not opposite ends of one scale.

Remember: Structure organizes the work. Consideration supports the person.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Naruto: training support changes with readiness

In an imagined training program, an eager beginner needs clear instructions: R1 telling. When the task feels harder than expected, provide direction plus support: R2 selling. As the group starts working well, emphasize support with less direction: R3 participating. When it is firing on all cylinders, step back: R4 delegating. These match your course’s readiness diagram.

Remember: Structure starts high, then drops. Consideration goes low → high → high → low.

Who controls the decision?

Decision styleControl
AutocraticLeader makes the decision
ConsultativeLeader asks for input, then decides
FacilitativeLeader supports a group decision and shared agreement
DelegativeEmployees take responsibility for making the decision

Time-sensitive model: choose a style based on decision significance, importance of commitment, leader expertise, likelihood of commitment, shared objectives, employee expertise and teamwork skills. There is no single best style. The slides report that leaders tend to overuse consultation and underuse autocratic and facilitative styles.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Soccer: who makes the lineup decision?

Imagine a coach chooses the lineup alone: autocratic. The coach asks Messi and Ronaldo for input but makes the final choice: consultative. The coach helps the group reach a shared decision: facilitative. The coach gives players responsibility for deciding: delegative. Asking for input does not automatically transfer decision authority.

Remember: Track who gets the final say.

The four I’s of transformational leadership

ElementCueDistinguish it from…
Idealized influenceAdmired role model; pride, respect, charismaA speech about the future
Inspirational motivation ★Compelling shared vision; enthusiasm about the futureIndividual coaching
Intellectual stimulationQuestion assumptions; consider new perspectivesSimply defining a deadline
Individualized considerationCoaching tailored to individual needs and aspirationsGeneral friendliness alone

The slides frame transformational leadership as inspiring meaningful commitment and helping followers reach their potential. For this course, slide 31 says the other three components can be trained, while charisma cannot.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Jujutsu Kaisen: four imagined moments with Gojo

Students feel proud to follow a respected role model: idealized influence. Gojo describes an exciting shared future: inspirational motivation. He asks them to question an old strategy: intellectual stimulation. He coaches Yuji around Yuji’s specific needs: individualized consideration. These are teaching examples, not a claim that every character action fits the model.

Remember: Role model / future vision / rethink / personal coaching.

Full range of leadership

Laissez-faire → Passive management-by-exception → Active management-by-exception → Contingent reward → Transformational

Laissez-faire: avoids leadership. Passive exception: waits for errors, then corrects. Active exception: monitors errors and corrects. Contingent reward: exchanges rewards for adequate performance. The diagram prioritizes the more active and effective approaches; the three middle approaches are transactional.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Dragon Ball: rewards are not the same as transformation

Imagine a trainer promises Goku a reward for completing a target: contingent reward, a transactional approach. Helping the whole group commit to a meaningful shared vision is transformational. Monitoring for mistakes is active management-by-exception; waiting for mistakes before intervening is passive management-by-exception.

Remember: Deal / shared meaning / monitor / react.

05 · Organizational culture

Culture, slides 4–30

Culture: shared social knowledge about rules, norms and values that shapes employees’ attitudes and behavior—“the way things are around here.”

LayerMeaningExample
Observable artifacts ★Visible or discussable manifestationsOpen office layout, logo, awards ceremony
Espoused valuesWhat the organization explicitly says it believes“We put customers first”
Basic underlying assumptions ★Deep, taken-for-granted beliefs, often unquestioned“You simply do not challenge the CEO”

Enacted values are reflected in actual behavior. A company may claim teamwork while rewarding only individual competition: espoused and enacted values differ.

ArtifactRecognition cue
SymbolsLogo or image
Physical structure ★Architecture or office layout
LanguageJargon, slang, slogans
StoriesAnecdotes, legends, accounts
RitualsRecurring daily/weekly routines
CeremoniesFormal events or celebrations
PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Naruto fan club: see it, say it, assume it

Imagine a campus Naruto club. Its banner and room layout are artifacts. “Everyone gets a voice” in its mission statement is an espoused value. An unquestioned belief that senior members must never be challenged is an underlying assumption. If seniors interrupt new members despite the slogan, the club has an espoused–enacted gap.

Remember: What you see / what they say / what nobody questions / what they actually do.

General culture types

Sociability: friendliness. Solidarity: thinking and acting alike.

Low solidarityHigh solidarity
High sociabilityNetworked: friendly, less alignedCommunal: friendly and aligned
Low sociabilityFragmented: less friendly, less alignedMercenary: less friendly, aligned

Specific cultures emphasize customer service, safety, diversity, sustainability, or creativity.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Soccer clubs: friendliness is not the same as alignment

Imagine four clubs. Friendly players who think and act alike: communal. Friendly players with little shared direction: networked. Less-friendly players who are strongly aligned: mercenary. Less-friendly players with little alignment: fragmented. “Mercenary” here is a course category, not a claim about a player’s motives.

Remember: Sociability = friendly. Solidarity = aligned.

Strong does not automatically mean good

Strength: employees agree about expectations and act consistently with them. Benefits include identity, distinctiveness, desired behavior and stability. Costs include harder mergers, less diversity of thought, extreme behavior and difficulty adapting.

Maintaining culture: ASA

Attraction → Selection → Attrition

Attraction ★: applicants choose organizations that fit them. Selection: organizations choose applicants who fit. Attrition: people who do not fit leave voluntarily or involuntarily.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Chainsaw Man fan club: who chooses whom?

Imagine you like a Chainsaw Man discussion club’s relaxed atmosphere and apply: attraction. Its officers choose applicants who fit its culture: selection. A member who dislikes the culture eventually leaves: attrition. Learning the club’s slang and routines after joining is socialization, a different process.

Remember: You choose them → they choose you → mismatched people leave.

Socialization: learning the culture

Newcomers learn goals/values, job proficiency, people, language, politics and history.

StageWhat happens
AnticipatoryBefore starting: build expectations
EncounterEntry into the workplace: compare expectations to reality
Understanding and adaptationLearn and internalize norms and expected behavior

Reality shock: expectations from before entry do not match the experience inside. Realistic job previews, orientation and mentoring help manage socialization.

Culture change: new leadership or mergers/acquisitions can change culture, but success is uncertain. A merger may produce conflicting or differentiated cultures rather than the unified culture managers hoped for.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Jujutsu Kaisen fan club: the first-week surprise

Imagine you expect casual anime chats before joining: anticipatory stage. At your first meeting, you discover strict debate rules: encounter. The gap between expectation and reality creates reality shock. Over time, you learn the norms and adapt: understanding and adaptation. A realistic preview beforehand could reduce the surprise.

Remember: Expect → encounter → adapt. A mismatch creates shock.

PICTURE THIS · IMAGINED STUDY SCENARIO

Ronaldo and Messi fan groups: strong does not mean right

Imagine merging two fan groups, each with deeply shared rules about how discussions should work. Each group’s strong culture creates belonging and predictable behavior, but their rules clash after the merger. That illustrates a cost of culture strength: integration and adaptation can be harder. Shared conviction does not guarantee good norms.

Remember: Strong = shared and consistently followed, not automatically good.
